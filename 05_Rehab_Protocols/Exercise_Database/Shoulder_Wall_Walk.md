## Basic info

- Name: Shoulder wall walk / wall walks / finger ladder
- Category: Range of Motion / shoulder flexion and abduction
- Exercise goal: gently restore overhead shoulder range using the wall as graded support, without forcing
- Source: Stone Clinic — Shoulder Wallwalk
- Video:

## Exercise idea

The user stands facing a wall and "walks" their fingers up the wall, letting the arm rise gradually. The wall carries part of the arm's weight, so the shoulder works in an assisted range. The aim is a smooth, controlled rise without shrugging or compensating with the torso.

## Reference performance

- Stand facing the wall, close enough to place the hand comfortably on it.
- Place the fingertips of the working hand on the wall at roughly chest height.
- Slowly walk the fingers up the wall, letting the arm rise.
- Keep the shoulder pulled down — don't shrug toward the ear.
- Keep the torso quiet — don't lean back or arch the lower back to gain height.
- Stop at the highest comfortable point; pause briefly.
- Walk the fingers back down under control.

## What to track

- pain before/after;
- maximum reached height (mark on the wall to compare across sessions);
- smoothness of the movement;
- shrugging / scapular elevation;
- torso compensation (leaning, lumbar arch);
- neck/trapezius tension;
- whether the range is increasing on trend.

## Common errors

- shrugging the shoulder to gain extra height;
- arching the lower back instead of moving from the shoulder;
- moving too fast and losing control;
- pushing through sharp pain;
- holding the breath during the climb.

## Importance

Cycle weight: `3` — important exercise for tracking overhead shoulder range with low load.

Why it matters:

- gives a simple, repeatable measurement (height on the wall) for week-over-week comparison;
- the wall provides graded support, so it's usable earlier than unsupported flexion;
- works well as a complement to supine shoulder flexion with a dowel.

## Restrictions and conditions

- Don't treat as universally allowed for all surgeries, injuries, and recovery timeframes.
- Add to a cycle only if it's in the personal protocol or the user has decided to track it themselves.
- Don't force range — stop at the first sharp pain or sense of instability.
- If the video/angle is poor — don't draw confident conclusions about technique.
